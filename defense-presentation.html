Slide 02 · General Context

When the camera becomes the sensor of action

Computer vision is no longer just a perception layer — it now closes the control loop. Visual servoing turns pixels into velocity commands, enabling robots to see, decide and act in milliseconds.

Vision-driven robotics

From assembly lines to surgical assistance, robots increasingly rely on cameras as their primary feedback channel — fast, cheap, and information-rich.

Closed-loop visual feedback

Visual servoing minimizes an error between current and desired views by computing a velocity twist vc = (v, ω) applied to the end-effector.

Configuration: Eye-in-Hand

The camera is rigidly mounted on the end-effector. It moves with the robot, providing the most direct mapping between motion and image change.

Slide 03 · Problem Statement

Pure photometric servoing is brilliant — and brittle

Direct PVS uses raw pixel intensities as features. No detection, no tracking — but the entire control law leans on a single fragile assumption: brightness constancy.

PB.01

Brightness constancy fails

The cost function $\sum (I - I^*)^2$ is only valid if illumination stays frozen between frames — a hypothesis broken by every shadow, reflection or auto-exposure tick.

PB.02

Dynamic illumination

Sun motion, lamp flicker and camera-gain control inject low-frequency drift that PVS misreads as a real geometric error, polluting the velocity command.

PB.03

Glare attacks

A sudden specular flash saturates pixels locally. Photometric residuals explode, the control loop oscillates and convergence is lost — sometimes catastrophically.

PVS error · pixel domain
$$ e_{\text{PVS}}(t) = \sum_{x,y} \big( I(x,y,t) - I^{*}(x,y) \big)^{2} $$
Brightness-constancy hypothesis (broken)
$$ I(x,y,t+\Delta t) \;\stackrel{?}{=}\; I(x+\Delta x,\, y+\Delta y,\, t) $$
Slide 04 · Objectives & Contributions

Three contributions, one robust controller

Re-express the photometric error in a multiscale wavelet space — keep the elegance of direct servoing, gain the resilience of multiresolution analysis.

C.01
Robustness

DWT-PVS

Replace raw intensities with the wavelet sub-bands of the image. Low-frequency illumination drift is isolated in the LL band and discarded; geometric content lives in the detail bands and drives the law.

C.02
Real-time

LWT-PVS

Use the Lifting Wavelet Transform — Split · Predict · Update — to compute the same decomposition with only additions and shifts. No multiplications: ideal for embedded loops at 25 FPS.

C.03
Strategy

Coarse-to-Fine Smart Switch

Start from the coarsest scale (large basin of attraction), then dynamically switch to finer scales as the error decreases. Best of both worlds: convergence range and pixel-level precision.

Slide 05 · Visual Servoing — A Quick Map

Three families, one control objective

All visual servos minimize an error e = s − s* via the interaction matrix Ls. The difference lies in the choice of feature s.

Image-Based

IBVS

s = 2D coordinates of geometric primitives (points, lines).

  • Robust to calibration errors
  • Requires feature extraction & tracking
  • Local minima in 3D space
Position-Based

PBVS

s = reconstructed 3D pose of the target.

  • Straight-line Cartesian motion
  • Needs accurate 3D model + calibration
  • Sensitive to noise on pose estimation
Direct / Photometric

PVS

s = entire image (every pixel intensity).

  • No extraction, no tracking — pure direct
  • Dense, sub-pixel accurate
  • Fragile under illumination changes
Generic control law
$$ \mathbf{v}_{c} \;=\; -\,\lambda \, \mathbf{L}_{s}^{+}\, \big( \mathbf{s}(t) - \mathbf{s}^{*} \big) $$
Slide 06 · Frequency Analysis & Its Limits

Why Fourier alone is the wrong lens for images

An image is a non-stationary 2D signal — its statistics change across regions. The Fourier Transform tells us which frequencies are present, but not where.

Global, not local

The basis functions $e^{-j\omega x}$ are infinite in extent: a single edge in the corner of an image perturbs the entire spectrum.

No spatial localization

Images contain abrupt transitions — edges, occlusions, glare spots. Fourier averages them away, losing the very information visual servoing needs.

The wavelet promise

Wavelets are localized in both space and frequency: they describe where a frequency exists. Perfect for non-stationary, edge-rich images.

Slide 07 · The Wavelet Solution

DWT & LWT — two views, one decomposition

A 2D image is split into four sub-bands at each level: one approximation (LL) and three details (LH, HL, HH). Iterating gives a multiresolution pyramid.

LL
Approximation · Illumination & coarse shape
HL
Vertical details · horizontal edges
LH
Horizontal details · vertical edges
HH
Diagonal details · corners & texture

By using the detail bands instead of raw intensities, we naturally filter out illumination drift living mostly in LL.

DWT

Discrete Wavelet Transform

Convolutional filter banks (low-pass + high-pass) followed by 2× down-sampling. Mathematically clean, but computationally heavy: many multiplications.

LWT

Lifting Wavelet Transform

The same transform — restructured as Split → Predict → Update. Uses only additions and shifts, runs in-place, and is perfectly invertible. Real-time friendly.

Slide 08 · Mathematical Modelling

DWT-PVS & LWT-PVS — error in the wavelet domain

Replace pixel intensities with their multiscale coefficients and re-derive the photometric cost. The control law inherits the same elegant form — only the feature has changed.

DWT-PVS · multiscale photometric error
$$ e_{\text{DWT}}(t) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{J} \, \alpha_{j} \!\!\sum_{(u,v) \in \mathcal{D}_{j}} \!\Big( W_{j}(u,v,t) - W_{j}^{*}(u,v) \Big)^{2} $$
$W_{j}$: detail coefficient at level $j$ · $\alpha_j$: scale weight · $\mathcal{D}_j$: detail bands {LH, HL, HH}.
LWT-PVS · with energy-normalization factor
$$ e_{\text{LWT}}(t) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{J} \, \frac{\alpha_{j}}{\mathcal{E}_{j}} \!\!\sum_{(u,v) \in \mathcal{D}_{j}} \!\Big( \tilde{W}_{j}(u,v,t) - \tilde{W}_{j}^{*}(u,v) \Big)^{2} \quad,\quad \mathcal{E}_{j} = \sum_{(u,v)} \tilde{W}_{j}^{*}(u,v)^{2} $$
The factor $\mathcal{E}_{j}$ compensates the non-orthogonality of the lifting scheme and preserves precision across scales.
Resulting control law
$$ \boxed{\;\mathbf{v}_{c} \;=\; -\,\lambda \, \mathbf{L}_{W}^{+}\, \big( \mathbf{W}(t) - \mathbf{W}^{*} \big)\;} $$
$\mathbf{L}_{W}$ is the wavelet interaction matrix, derived analytically from the spatial gradients of the sub-bands.
Slide 09 · Control Architecture & Smart Switch

A 25 FPS closed-loop controller with adaptive scale

Implemented on top of the ViSP framework with a 0.04 s sampling period. The Smart Switch dynamically migrates from coarse (Low-Low) to fine (high-frequency) bands as the error converges.

Smart Switch

Adaptive coarse-to-fine

The controller starts on the LL band of the deepest level — large convergence basin. As ‖e‖ drops below a threshold, it switches to higher-frequency bands for sub-pixel accuracy.

Safety

Velocity saturation

The output twist is clamped to a safe norm vmax — protecting both the manipulator and the workspace during initial transients.

Implementation

ViSP & OpenCV

The full pipeline runs on standard hardware via the ViSP library, with a 0.04 s sample period. LWT keeps the loop budget well below the deadline.

Slide 11 · Conclusion & Perspectives

A direct visual servo that is robust, fast, and adaptive

By moving the photometric error into a multiscale wavelet space and adding an adaptive smart switch, we keep the simplicity of direct PVS — and gain the resilience needed for real-world robotics.

Take-aways

What we delivered

  • A wavelet-domain reformulation of photometric servoing
  • An LWT variant with multiplication-free real-time cost
  • A coarse-to-fine smart switch widening the basin of attraction
  • Demonstrated robustness to glare attacks & illumination drift
Next steps

Perspectives

  • Real robot validation on a 6-DoF eye-in-hand platform
  • Learning-based weighting of sub-bands ($\alpha_j$ as neural output)
  • Extension to colour and event-based cameras
  • Deeper Lifting schemes (CDF 9/7) for finer detail capture
