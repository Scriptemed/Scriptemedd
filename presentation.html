SECTION 01 · INTRODUCTION
Problem Statement

Pure photometric servoing breaks under real-world light.

Photometric Visual Servoing (PVS) uses raw pixel intensities as visual features. Powerful — but fragile when its core assumption is violated.

// limitation 01

Brightness Constancy

PVS strictly assumes that pixel intensities remain constant across views. Reality rarely respects this assumption.

// limitation 02

Dynamic Illumination

Shadows, moving light sources, exposure changes and reflections rapidly destabilise the control law.

// limitation 03

Glare Attacks

Localised saturations and specular highlights inject huge errors directly into the cost function.

e(t) = I(r(t)) − I*   // pixel-level error
⚠ assumes ∂I/∂t = 0

A small illumination drift produces a large, biased photometric error → the control law diverges or oscillates, even when the geometric scene is unchanged.

SECTION 02 · THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS
Visual Servoing Concepts

Three families · one closed loop.

Visual servoing schemes differ by the space in which the error is defined — image, pose, or directly the photometric signal itself.

// Approach A

IBVS

Image-Based Visual Servoing — error computed on extracted geometric features (points, lines, moments) in the image plane.

+ Robust to calibration · − Needs feature extraction
// Approach B

PBVS

Position-Based Visual Servoing — error computed in 3D Cartesian space after estimating the camera pose.

+ Geometrically intuitive · − Sensitive to pose estimation noise
// Direct approach

PVS

Photometric Visual Servoing — uses raw pixel intensities directly. No feature extraction. No matching.

+ Dense information · − Sensitive to illumination
IBVS: e = s(I) − s*
PBVS: e = [t, θu]
PVS: e = I − I*
our framework targets the direct PVS family

SECTION 02 · THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS
Frequency Analysis & its Limits

Fourier knows what — but not where.

The Fourier transform decomposes a signal globally, losing the spatial location of its frequency components — a critical issue for non-stationary signals like images.

F(ω) = ∫ f(x) · e−jωx dx
  • Global basis — every Fourier coefficient depends on the entire signal.
  • No spatial localization — a sharp edge "smears" across the frequency spectrum.
  • Non-stationarity — natural images are full of local discontinuities (contours, textures).
  • STFT partially helps but suffers from a fixed window size Heisenberg trade-off
→ we need a basis that is both frequency-aware and spatially localized.

The Wavelet Solution · DWT · CWT · LWT

A localised, multiscale lens on the image.

  • CWT · Continuous Wavelet Transform — analyses every scale and position; mathematically rich, redundant.
  • DWT · Discrete Wavelet Transform — separates each level into LL (illumination trends) and LH/HL/HH (structural detail). image filter banks
  • LWT · Lifting Wavelet Transform — a spatial-domain reformulation: Split → Predict → Update
  • No complex multiplications · in-place computation · ideal for real-time control. 2× faster typical

Mathematical Modeling · DWT-PVS & LWT-PVS

The error migrates from pixels to wavelet coefficients.

Instead of comparing raw intensities, we compare wavelet coefficients — naturally separating illumination from structure.

DWT-PVS · photometric error in wavelet domain

eDWT(t) = W{I(r(t))} − W{I*}
ė = LW · v  // wavelet interaction matrix
v = −λ · LW+ · eDWT

LWT-PVS · energy-normalised lifting variant

eLWT = κ · ( L{I} − L{I*} )
κ = energy normalisation factor
κ preserves the photometric precision lost by the lifting reordering.
